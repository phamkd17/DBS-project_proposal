# User Interviews

Goal: at least 3 people, in person or on a call. Ask about their experience
("Tell me about the last time you..."). Don't describe the app until the end.
Write down what they said **in their words**.

## How to use this bank

- Pick **5–8 questions** per person. Don't run the whole list.
- Start with a warm-up, then go where their answers are most interesting.
- Ask about **real past events** ("the last time…"), not hypotheticals ("would
  you…?"). People are bad at predicting what they'd do.
- Don't pitch. If they ask what you're building, say "I'll tell you at the end."
- Silence is fine. Let them fill it.

### Follow-up probes (use with any question)

- "What happened next?"
- "Why do you think that happened?"
- "Can you show me?" (the screenshot, the notebook, the text thread)
- "How did that feel?"
- "What did you try instead?"
- "Was that the only time, or does that happen a lot?"
- "You said ___ — what do you mean by that?"

## A. Family members who want to recreate dishes (cousins, aunt, me)

### Warm-up

1. How often do you cook in a normal week? What did you make most recently?
2. Where do your recipes usually come from?
3. What's a dish you grew up eating that you'd love to be able to make yourself?

### Recreating family dishes

4. Tell me about the last time you tried to make a dish someone in the family makes. What happened?
5. When you want to know how Grandma makes something, what do you do? Walk me through the last time.
6. When you've asked her how she makes something, what did she actually say?
7. Has anyone in the family ever written down one of her recipes? Where is it now?
8. Tell me about a time the family talked about a dish nobody knew how to make anymore.
9. When you tried to make one of her dishes, how close did it come? How could you tell?
10. What's the hardest part of learning a dish from someone who doesn't measure?

### Following recipes today

11. Walk me through the last time you cooked from a recipe, from finding it to eating it.
12. Tell me about the last time a recipe didn't turn out the way you expected. What did you do next?
13. When a step doesn't make sense, what do you do in that moment?
14. Tell me about the last time you changed a recipe to suit your taste. What did you change, and how did you decide?
15. When was the last time you looked something up mid-cook? What were you looking for, and where did you look?
16. Have you ever skipped looking something up because your hands were messy or you were in a hurry? What happened?
17. How do you know when something is "done" if the recipe says "until golden" or "until translucent"?

### Keeping track

18. Where do you keep the recipes you want to make again? Can you show me?
19. Tell me about the last time you couldn't find a recipe you'd saved.
20. Have you ever made a dish a few times and changed it each time? How did you remember what you changed?

### Inspiration

21. Tell me about the last time you saw a dish or technique you wanted to try later. What did you do with it?
22. Did you ever come back to it? What got in the way, or what helped?
23. Where do you get cooking ideas: restaurants, social media, friends, cookbooks?

### Sharing

24. Tell me about the last time you shared a recipe with someone. How did you send it?
25. Tell me about the last time someone shared one with you. Did you make it?

## B. Grandma (the recipe holder)

Tip: do this in the kitchen if you can, and record audio with her permission.
The recording becomes the first entry in the eval set.

### Warm-up

1. What did you cook this week?
2. What's the dish the family asks you for most?
3. Who taught you to cook? How did they teach you?

### How she cooks

4. How did you learn to make your yogurt? Has the way you make it changed over the years?
5. When you make flan, how do you know it's ready?
6. When you make tomato tofu, how do you know when it tastes right?
7. How do you decide how much sugar or salt to add?
8. What happens when you don't have an ingredient you usually use?
9. Have you ever made a dish and it didn't come out right? What happened? How did you fix it?
10. What's something about one of your dishes that most people get wrong?
11. Is there a step you never skip? Why?

### Passing recipes on

12. Tell me about the last time someone asked you how to make one of your dishes. What did you tell them?
13. Have you ever watched someone try to make one of your dishes? How did it go?
14. Have you ever written a recipe down, or had someone write one down for you? What happened to it?
15. Which of your dishes would you most want the grandkids to be able to make?
16. When you explain a dish, what's the hardest part to put into words?

### Phone and technology

17. What do you use your phone for on a normal day?
18. Have you ever sent someone a voice message? Who, and about what?
19. Tell me about the last time you took a picture of food.
20. Is there anything on your phone that's frustrating to use?
21. When you're cooking, where is your phone?

## C. Aunt (helper who writes things down for Grandma)

## D. Beginner cooks (friends) — optional, for later users

1. Tell me about the last thing you cooked. How did you pick it?
2. Walk me through the last time you followed a recipe step by step.
3. Tell me about a time you didn't understand why a recipe asked you to do something.

5. Where do you go when you want to learn a cooking technique? Tell me about the last time.

## Wrap-up (end of every interview — now you can describe the idea)

1. Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?
2. *(Describe the idea in one or two sentences.)* What's your first reaction?
3. What would make this not worth using?
4. Who else should I talk to?

## Notes

### Interview 1 — Aunt

**Has anyone in the family ever written down one of her recipes? Where is it now?**

> Yes, there is Grandma's recipe book, which your youngest aunts used to transcribe
> for her. Everything was handwritten, and it covers the past 20–30 years, since she
> moved to the US. It contains just ingredients and very simple steps for creating
> certain dishes that your grandma has experimented with and learned from friends
> and family. There's a notebook that we keep in the pantry. I think one of your
> cousins took pictures of it to digitize the old notes.

**What's the hardest part of learning a dish from someone who doesn't measure?**

> The hardest part of learning from someone who doesn't measure precisely is
> understanding their taste preferences and their intuition about how much of an
> ingredient is in the cooking vessel versus how much is needed. It's also about
> understanding the ratios of different ingredients. For example, if basil is very
> strong, how much do you use? Fresh basil is also very strong, but when you cook
> it, you lose some of its aroma. How do you adjust for that and the change in
> flavor? The hardest part is understanding what's going through the person's mind
> as they adjust the ingredients and techniques while cooking and balance the
> different flavors.

**Tell me about the last time a recipe didn't turn out the way you expected. What did you do next?**

> When a recipe doesn't turn out the way I expect, I just eat it. It's just extra
> calories, but I take detailed notes on what I want it to be and what I need to
> change the next time I make it so that it becomes what I want. I can always
> adjust the five basic tastes—sweet, sour, umami, bitter, and salty—until they
> match my preferred ratio, but the flavors might still be off.

**Have you ever skipped looking something up because your hands were messy or you were in a hurry? What happened?**

> Absolutely. When my hands are dirty and I forget how to do something, I just wing
> it and hope for the best. Sometimes that causes issues if I'm following a precise
> recipe, like when baking. If I use too much or too little baking soda, or knead
> the bread too much, it becomes something I eat for calories rather than a meal I
> really enjoy. But as I learn more about cooking, I get better at winging it and
> achieving good results.

**How do you know when something is "done" if the recipe says "until golden" or "until translucent"?**

> When I first learned how to cook, I tried different things until I understood
> how they affected the flavor. For example, for 'until golden,' I would cook
> something for 3, 5, 7, or 10 minutes, then look at the color and taste how each
> stage imparted different flavors to the dish. Garlic is a great example. Golden
> garlic is toasty and sweet, but if you go a little further, you get some
> crispiness with a slight hint of bitterness. If you go too far, it becomes
> bitter, even if it isn't burned yet. Conversely, if you want more of the raw,
> garlicky bite, you cook it only until translucent. It's all about experimenting.

### Interview 2 — Grandma

**How did you learn to make your yogurt? Has the way you make it changed over the years?**

> I learned to make yogurt from one of my friends. Your grandfather likes it sour,
> so I let it ferment a little longer than the original recipe calls for. The
> tanginess makes my yogurt unique. Before, it was too sweet for the family's taste
> preferences, so I learned about the different components that make yogurt what
> it is and tweaked it so the family would like it best.

**When you make tomato tofu, how do you know when it tastes right?**

> I like to make tomato tofu extremely flavorful. I deep-fry the tofu to make it
> extra spongy so that it can soak up all the tomato sauce and meat broth,
> flavoring it from the inside out while the tofu itself provides a slightly creamy
> texture. I make sure the fish sauce is caramelized to eliminate the fishy flavor,
> and I add a little sugar to cut the acidity of the tomatoes. Sometimes I don't
> even use sugar; I just caramelize the onions a little longer to bring out their
> natural sweetness and use that as a component of the soup instead of adding
> sugar. Then I add salt and a little garlic for a spicy kick. Sometimes I put Thai
> chilies in at the very end if I want that fresh, peppery flavor. There's really
> no right or wrong when it comes to what tastes right. It's just whatever I'm in
> the mood for.

**What happens when you don't have an ingredient you usually use?**

> Either I go without it or I substitute something else. For example, if I have a
> dish that uses lemon or lime and I don't have either, I might add vinegar because
> the main qualities I'm looking for are freshness and sourness. I can recreate the
> sourness by adding vinegar, tamarind, or something else with a sour component,
> even though the flavor might not work quite right. To me, if you can balance the
> five tastes—salty, umami, sweet, bitter, and sour—the dish is edible. The key is
> balancing those five.

**Have you ever made a dish and it didn't come out right? What happened? How did you fix it?**

> There are many times when I experiment with a dish and it doesn't come out the
> way I think it should. I try to fix it by balancing the five tastes to my liking.
> For example, if a dish is too sweet, I try to add a little more sourness and
> saltiness to offset it. Even if I don't like it, I try to eat it anyway because I
> don't like wasting food. I take notes on how to improve it.

**Have you ever written a recipe down or had someone write one down for you? What happened to it?**

> I keep a little logbook of all the dishes I've tried to make, and your aunt
> sometimes transcribes it into a new book for me. Sometimes I have pictures and
> notes on my phone to capture my experiments, but I mostly use voice recordings
> when I'm cooking because my hands get dirty. I talk to the phone so your aunt
> knows what to write down later.

### Interview 3 — Cousin

**Have you ever made one of her dishes from what you wrote down? How did it turn out?**

> I tried making Grandma's yogurt, and it never really tasted the same. Her
> ingredient list was just a jug of milk and a small carton of yogurt. I didn't
> realize that I needed to buy yogurt with live, active cultures, so I tried a
> bunch of different brands, but they still never tasted the same. I tried
> everything from very cheap brands to very fancy ones, but the flavor was
> different from what I remembered. Then I realized it had to be the specific
> yogurt container with the bacterial strain that made it taste the way Grandma
> made it when we were kids. That was the secret ingredient. It was interesting to
> realize that it's only a three-ingredient item, but the wide variety of brands
> and ingredients available at the market can really change how something tastes.
> In the end, there's just milk, yogurt starter, sugar, heat, and time.

**Tell me about the last time you saw a dish or technique you wanted to try later. What did you do with it?**

> I see inspiration on social media and YouTube all the time, and I eat out at
> restaurants a lot. Whenever a dish inspires me, I take note of it and try to
> think of ways to recreate it. For example, I'd never had Caesar salad with
> anchovies before, and I was fascinated by the complexity of the flavors when it
> wasn't just bottled Caesar dressing. There was a creamy umami intensity balanced
> by the satisfying, refreshing crispness of the lettuce. I took pictures of it:
> just a simple Caesar salad with white anchovies. I wanted to capture it and
> figure out how to make it. It was literally just a picture on my phone of a
> Caesar salad with white anchovies, along with a menu description from the
> restaurant where I ordered it, so that I would remember to investigate and try
> to recreate something like it.

**Where do you get cooking ideas: restaurants, social media, friends, cookbooks?**

> I usually get inspiration from restaurants, social media, friends, and
> experimenting at the grocery market: what are all the different ingredients, and
> how do they interact with each other? An unusual ingredient is what gets me
> really excited.

**Tell me about the last time you couldn't find a recipe you'd saved.**

> All the time, because I take pictures and have random notepads and files on my
> phone, documents on my computer, and notes in all different places. Sometimes I
> think I might have written something down, but I don't remember where it is. I
> just give up looking for it because finding it is more time-consuming than
> recreating something.

**When you want to know how Grandma makes something, what do you do? Walk me through the last time.**

> I usually just ask her how to make it. For example, she made a potato and beef
> stir-fry with broccoli, and it was very good. Most Vietnamese dishes have a very
> strong fish sauce flavor, but for whatever reason, hers didn't. I asked her how
> she made her potatoes and stir-fry, and she told me that you have to caramelize
> the sugars first, then caramelize the fish sauce. She explained that if you
> don't caramelize the fish sauce, it still has proteins that haven't been
> denatured, which gives it that fishy flavor. Once you heat it past the
> caramelization point without burning it, it has an intense umami saltiness that
> goes very well with onions, tomatoes, broccoli, potatoes, and all those good
> things. So I learn by talking to Grammy.

## Synthesis — what I learned

1. **The workflow already exists — it's just manual and lossy.** Grandma already
   talks to her phone while cooking *"because my hands get dirty,"* so the aunt
   *"knows what to write down later."* The aunt transcribes into a handwritten
   notebook (20–30 years of recipes, kept in the pantry). A cousin photographed
   it. → The app doesn't need to teach Grandma a new habit. It replaces the
   voice memo → aunt → notebook chain.

2. **What's lost in transcription is the *why*.** The notebook has *"just
   ingredients and very simple steps."* The cousin's yogurt never tasted right
   because the list said *"a jug of milk and a small carton of yogurt"* — nothing
   about live cultures or the specific brand. Grandma ferments longer because
   Grandpa *"likes it sour"* — also not written down. → Validates the two-layer
   design and flagged ambiguities (e.g. *"which yogurt brand? does it say live
   and active cultures?"*).

3. **Everyone reasons with the five tastes.** Grandma: *"The key is balancing
   those five."* The aunt adjusts *"the five basic tastes … until they match my
   preferred ratio."* → The "why" layer can be organized around the five tastes:
   what each step adds, and how to rebalance (*"too sweet → add sour and salt"*).

4. **Recipes are a set of dials, not one path.** Tomato tofu: sugar *or* longer
   caramelized onions; Thai chilies *"if I want that fresh, peppery flavor."*
   *"There's really no right or wrong."* Substitutions are by function: no lime →
   vinegar or tamarind, *"because the main qualities I'm looking for are freshness
   and sourness."* → The recipe should store variations and substitutions, not a
   single version.

5. **The hard part is the cook's mind.** Aunt: *"understanding what's going through
   the person's mind as they adjust the ingredients."* → Capture her reasoning
   alongside her steps.

6. **Mid-cook friction is real.** Aunt: *"When my hands are dirty and I forget how
   to do something, I just wing it and hope for the best."* → Confirms the
   hypothesis from Q2.

7. **Everyone already keeps a trial log.** Grandma and the aunt both *"take notes on
   how to improve it"* after a miss. → Strong evidence for the trial logbook (v2).

8. **Inspiration gets lost.** Cousin: notes in *"random notepads and files … in all
   different places … I just give up looking for it."* Their capture is a photo +
   the menu description. → Validates inspiration capture (v2) and its shape:
   photo + text + "investigate later."

9. **Grandma already explains science — in her own words.** Caramelizing the fish
   sauce *"to eliminate the fishy flavor."* → Keep *her* reasoning and the
   *app's* added science visibly separate, and check the added science in evals.
