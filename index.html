Culinary Lab Book

Culinary Lab Book helps cooks turn what they say while they cook into recipes that can be shared for others to repeat.

“Do you remember the yogurt Grandma made?” — my cousins and me, many times. No one knows how to recreate it.

The problem

My grandma cooks every day without measuring. She already talks to her phone while she cooks, and my aunt writes it down later in a notebook in the pantry. But the notebook has “just ingredients and very simple steps.” The amounts, the reasons, and her adjustments get lost.

When my cousin tried her yogurt from that list, it “never really tasted the same.” The list didn't say the starter needed live cultures, or which brand she used.

How it works, with Grandma's yogurt

A mockup of what v1 should do, using her real dictation.

  1. Grandma's words

    She talks while she cooks

    Her phone's dictation types it out. It's saved exactly as she said it, mistakes included.

    “…one of these small 10 in yogurt container that is not flavored at all uh you heat up the milk make sure it doesn't boil uh just until it steams a little bit and then from there you add 1/3 a cup of sugar…”

  2. The app asks

    Before writing the recipe, it asks what it can't know

    • “Small 10 in yogurt container”: is that Dannon? Plain, with live and active cultures?
      Grandma Yes, plain Dannon.
    • She goes straight from steaming milk to adding yogurt. Does she let it cool first?
      Grandma Yes.

    She knew both answers. She just left them out, and that's exactly what the app has to catch.

    If no one knows, the app offers a suggestion, labeled so it's never mistaken for hers.

  3. The recipe

    A recipe the family can repeat, with the why

    Heat ½ gallon of milk until it steams. Don't boil.
    Food scienceHeating changes the milk proteins, so the yogurt sets thicker.
    Let it cool before stirring in one small cup of plain Dannon.
    Food scienceMilk that's too hot kills the live cultures, and the yogurt won't set.
    SuggestedAbout 110°F: warm, but comfortable to touch.
    Incubate 8 hours for mild, 12 hours for super tangy.
    Grandma“Your grandfather likes it sour, so I let it ferment a little longer.”

  4. Shared with the family

    A cousin who's never made it follows the recipe, Grandma tastes it, and says it's hers. That's the test.

Who it's for, and what they told me

v1 is for my grandma and me: she's an expert cook who isn't tech-savvy, and I'm good with technology. I interviewed her, my aunt, and a cousin.

Grandma · the recipe holder

“I mostly use voice recordings when I'm cooking because my hands get dirty. I talk to the phone so your aunt knows what to write down later.”

“To me, if you can balance the five tastes—salty, umami, sweet, bitter, and sour—the dish is edible. The key is balancing those five.”

My aunt · writes it down for her

“The hardest part is understanding what's going through the person's mind as they adjust the ingredients and techniques while cooking and balance the different flavors.”

“When my hands are dirty and I forget how to do something, I just wing it and hope for the best.”

My cousin · trying to recreate her dishes

“I tried making Grandma's yogurt, and it never really tasted the same. Her ingredient list was just a jug of milk and a small carton of yogurt.”

“I just give up looking for it because finding it is more time-consuming than recreating something.”

What I learned

  • The habit already exists. Grandma already dictates while she cooks. The app replaces the phone → aunt → notebook chain; it doesn't ask her to learn something new.
  • What's lost is the why. The notebook keeps ingredients and steps, but not her reasons or the details that matter, like the yogurt starter.
  • Recipes are dials, not one path. She changes dishes for who's eating and what she has, and reasons in the five tastes.
  • Everyone already keeps notes on what to change next time, and inspiration gets lost across photos and notes apps. Those come in v2.

Focus: AI

Turning Grandma's messy speech into recipes is the hard part, and I want to learn how to better leverage AI. Speech-to-text is solved; her phone already does it. Understanding what she meant is not.

I'm building evals from three of her real dictations (yogurt, tomato tofu, pork mango soup). They check that the app:

  • Asks instead of guessing when it can't know, like fish sauce measured as “just about that much.”
  • Catches missing steps, like cooling the milk before the yogurt goes in.
  • Fixes dictation errors without touching her words, like “a 32nd” → 30 seconds.
  • Labels every suggestion, so her amounts and the AI's never get mixed up.
